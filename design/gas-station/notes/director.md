# Director notes — gas station portfolio

## State

- Phase: jam, pass 1, turn 3 done (systems, visual, then me). No concepts yet. No scores given.
- The product has no brand or visual foundation. The owner has not answered any questions yet. Anything below marked "lean" is mine, not the owner's.
- Settled by the owner's words only: portfolio; vintage gas station; desert; monocolour black and white brush strokes; start outside, can go in; Easter eggs ("alike stuff", probably alien, unconfirmed; time of day); "Ed Ruscha meets Jack Kirby's sketches"; liminal; every interaction has artistic meaning; leaning three.js.

## What I looked at

All 18 inspiration images and `sketches/ink-is-shadow/sheet.png`.

- Sketch: frames 4, 5, 6 (night outside, inside by day, inside at night) are strong because ink is mass. Frame 1 (desktop day, frontal) reads as an outline filter on boxes. Frame 2's empty sky is the most Ruscha thing on the sheet.
- Inspo splits I noticed and asked the owner about: pure ink (13, 14) versus graphite haze (04, 07); plain box stations (02, 09, 10) versus streamlined/Googie (01, 03, 12).

## My steers in the jam (leans, for the designers to build on)

1. "Ink is where the light isn't" is the foundation, on three conditions: mass before line (lost edges on the lit side, outline only for near things and events); shadows laid as real tapered strokes in the light's direction (light says where, hand says how; joins the visual designer's S1 and S2); the rule carries meaning, since ink is also time.
2. Ruscha/Kirby split: keep, add a second axis. In time: rest is Ruscha, contact is Kirby. In space: outside is Ruscha (empty), inside is Kirby (dense, built, deep). Landed words stay deadpan Ruscha slabs. Trap to avoid: Kirby as bursts and sound effects, which makes it comic cosplay. The owner said "sketches" and attached original boards.
3. Phone: tall picture designed first; big sky for arrival. Panels are not the world's layout. Panels kept alive for one use only: a gutter is time (four tiers, same station at four hours).
4. Shape B (two-sided strip) becomes the plain list, a paste-up strip of every piece.
5. Door: a latch, one deliberate second, never a puzzle; links and the list skip it.
6. Loop: keep the geometry (out the back, round to the front), but a lap must not change the hour. Real time moves the sun; the wall clock is the one place a visitor moves it.

## Directions I proposed

- **The station is a sundial** (from a real random draw: "sundial making"). Sign pole is the gnomon, the lot is the dial, the shadow points at the piece of the hour, the name is cast on the ground and grows toward dusk, the wall clock inside turns the sun, eggs are alignments, night has no hand. Gives a second tall-phone arrival: the ground does the work instead of the sky. Shadow recommends, never locks.
- **The visitor is the sighting** (depends on "alien"). The person on the site is the thing that arrives unannounced. Krackle marks their contact. The sightings chart logs their visits. Evidence only, never a creature on screen.
- From Oblique Strategy "Look closely at the most embarrassing details and amplify them": the name as large and flat as STANDARD; the price board as a real price board, each number a true fact about the piece; About as what the absent attendant left behind.

## Guardrails I set

Deadpan test (no winking, no Route 66 kitsch); nothing responds except by ink; a small number of composed views, each printable; the work is always the lit thing; keep the vocabulary small (five gestures, three type voices, four hours).

## Objections I expect from the owner (none tested yet)

- "It looks like a filter on a 3D model", if outlines and procedural edges survive. This is the largest risk.
- "I said brush strokes", if the hand isn't visibly real. Depends on whether Adam makes the marks.
- "It's a theme park", if the alien and roadside material winks.
- "I can't find the work", if the place gets in the way. Systems' rule guards this.
- Strict two-value may be wrong: his Ruscha images are full of grey haze. Asked.
- I'm at risk of over-loving the sundial. When concepts come, check it against two pieces of work and against night, and be hard on it.

## Open questions to the owner

Asked earlier by others, unanswered: what goes in the portfolio and how much; who it's for; "alike" = alien?; work in colour or monochrome; music as portfolio, atmosphere, both or neither; will Adam make the brush marks; station name; stepped views or free movement; toned paper as "white".

Mine, new:
1. Strictly ink or paper per pixel, or is grey haze allowed?
2. Which brush: 13, 14, or Kirby's inked line?
3. Which station: plain box (02, 09, 10) or streamlined (01, 03, 12)?
4. How straight-faced: deadpan evidence or funny out loud?
5. Place first or work first on arrival?
6. Whose name is on the sign, and how large?

## What blocks a 9

Nothing scored yet. Before scoring I need at least: the two-value answer, which brush, and whether Adam makes the marks. Without those I can't judge any concept against the owner's "brush strokes".

## Next

Pass 2 of the jam. I asked systems to lay out the lot as a dial and test it at two pieces and at forty; visual to redo one frame as mass without outline and to try the two tall arrivals side by side; both to find where the sundial breaks, starting with night.

# After jam pass 2 (jam closed)

## State

- Jam is over. Next: the two designers build 2–4 concepts that differ in kind; I steer after each pair of turns, then score.
- Still no owner answers beyond "I'm thinking threejs". All steers provisional. No scores given yet.

## What I looked at

`sketches/dial-on-a-phone/sheet.png` (ten tall frames, 2D, generated strokes, real sun at 35°N).

- Stroke rule (no outline, shadow laid along the light) works as a picture. Not proven as a real hand or in three.js.
- Night frames 6 and 10 are the strongest things drawn so far.
- Frame 9 (eye height, raking beam overhead) is the best day picture, and the dial isn't readable in it.
- Frames 3, 4, 5, 7, 8 are diagrams: station is a slot, a tenth of the screen, seen from 7 m up.
- Two passes in, nobody has drawn the interior, a piece held, or the list.

## Self-check on my favourite

I proposed the sundial and the team spent a whole pass on its geometry. Risk: a clever instrument the visitor has to decode, with the gas station (the owner's actual subject) pushed to the back. Condition I set: it must be felt by someone who never works out it's a dial. When scoring, hold The Instrument to that and don't reward the geometry for being true.

## Rulings

- Raking beam over flat stencil, where there is a dial. Flat stencil dropped (name mirrored in the air). But the beam decides "which station", which is the owner's call, so it lives in one concept only.
- No length floor, no cheats with the sun.
- Camera riding the hour: allowed as four composed pictures with easing between (twilight, morning/evening, midday, night). Station must be the subject in each. The high midday view on the sheet fails this; lower it.
- Twilight points at nothing. Yes.
- Agreed: light source sets stroke direction; two blacks (brushed shadow, flat paint); stars with tails versus krackle dots; timetable with fixed order and a bar at "now"; links never carry the hour; wound clock marked and released on leaving.
- Caution given to systems: turns-by-date, queues and stamped receipts are a lot of machinery for a possibly small portfolio. Keep what's felt in one visit.

## Directions carried into concepts

1. **The Instrument** — beam, painted fan, timetable, sky at night; streamlined station; time chooses the work; idea lives outside. Must prove: station stays the subject, works unnoticed, holds at 3 and 30 pieces, inside as strong as outside.
2. **Nobody's here** — plain box, upright sign, frontal, eye height, no dial; time only changes light and oddities; idea lives at the door and inside (Ruscha out, Kirby in, work lit, attendant's things as About, visitor as the sighting). Must prove: more than a lit shop, the door is an event, absence is felt.
3. **The board** (optional) — paste-up, flats, margin notes, attention as inker, four tiers for four hours; least 3D. Must prove: "go in" is still an event; not a comic.
A fourth of the designers' own is welcome if different in kind.

Shared rules for all, and five moments every concept must show on a tall phone: arrive, cross the door, inside with work lit, one piece held, the list.

## Dropped

Flat stencil sign; floored shadow; high camera where the station is a slot; panels as world layout; lap changing the hour; list reordering; hour in links; cast name as a requirement; my own "shadow toward you from the front".

## Can't judge a concept without (owner)

1. Which brush, and will Adam make the marks? (Largest unproven thing.)
2. Strict ink/paper or grey haze?
3. Which station: box or streamlined? (Concepts 1 and 2 show one each.)
4. What goes in and how much?
5. Place first or work first?
6. "Alike" = "alien"?

New question asked this turn: should the time of day choose which work is shown, or only change how the place looks and what odd things happen?

Until 1–3 at least are answered I can rank concepts as ideas but not call any a 9.

## Expected owner objections, updated

- "These aren't brush strokes" — generated hatching combs like grass on walls.
- "It's a filter / it's a comic / it's a theme park" as before.

- Is the work drawn? Interior first, not more exterior.
- Does each concept differ in kind, or are 1 and 2 drifting into the same site with different signs?
- Is machinery growing past what one visit can feel?
