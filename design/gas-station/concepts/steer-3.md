## What this is and isn't

**It is a change to the look, not to the concept.** He has said nothing against the place, the sign, the walking, the lamp and shadow, or things left on. He walked it twice and called the feel "pretty good and fluid". Left on stands. `05-left-on.md`'s how-it-works half doesn't change.

**But one of the drawing's rules was wrong, and it was mine.** In the jam I set "mass before line" and called outline "the tell of a filter". Every round since has made tone out of two flat areas with a ragged border between them. That is what posterized means: a picture reduced to a couple of flat levels. He has now said it twice in different words. The rule "ink is where the light isn't" stays. **How ink makes tone has to change.**

Two things in his message are defects in the prototype, separate from the look, and both are real:
- **Pixellated.** I never saw it because I only ever looked in a headless browser at low density.
- **Press and hold brings up copy and paste.** Press and hold is how you walk. Nobody tested touch on a real phone.

## What I see in his pictures now that the prototype doesn't have

I went back through the inspiration with "posterized", "texturing" and "crosshatching" in mind.

- **16 and 17 (Kirby covers).** There is almost no flat black in them. Forms are made of **line**: a contour with real weight, heavy on the shadow side and thin on the lit side, and inside it rows of short strokes that feather from black into white. The Thing in 17 is entirely a texture of cells. The street in 16 is brick, window, rivet, cable. **Every surface says what it is made of.** A lot of paper is left, and the eye reads tone from how close the lines are.
- **18 (Silver Surfer page).** The big blacks are there, and none has a ragged edge. They end in **feathering**: tapered strokes combed out of the black. Between black and white there are dots, speed lines and wash. Count the kinds of mark on that one page: solid, feather, hatch, dot cluster, radiating line, contour. The prototype has two: solid, and ragged edge.
- **13 (the brush eye).** Tone is **inside** the stroke. A dry brush leaves streaks of paper within the mark, so a single stroke goes from loaded to nearly empty. Strokes follow the form: round the eye, along the lashes. There are at least four weights of mark, from a hairline to a blot.
- **14.** The one reference that is flat black shapes. We built the whole look on it. It is one picture of eighteen.
- **04 and 07 (Ruscha).** Even his "flat" pictures aren't flat. HONK is a continuous graphite gradient. Hollywood in the Rain is tone made of fine speckle, lighter and darker across the sheet. The letters are hard; the world is soft.
- **15 (the sightings chart).** Thin even line, and it uses stipple and hatching for the dark parts.

What that adds up to: **in his pictures, darkness is a matter of degree, and the degrees are made of marks.** Close hatching, crossed hatching, feathering, stipple, dry-brush streaks, then solid black last and least. In ours, a place is either paper or ink, decided by a cut-off.

I also read his sentence "Ed Ruscha meets Jack Kirby's sketches" too far toward Ruscha. He wrote *sketches*, and three of his images are Kirby's boards. The emptiness and stillness are Ruscha's. The drawing is Kirby's.

## What "not posterized" has to mean here

Outcomes, not methods. How you get there is yours.

1. **Many steps of tone, all made of marks.** Between bare paper and solid black a viewer should be able to find at least four distinct darknesses in one frame, and tell them apart by the marks: open hatching, close hatching, crosshatching, near-solid. A shadow gets darker by gaining lines, not by a border moving.
2. **A shadow's edge is where its lines stop.** No ragged blob outlines anywhere. If you squint at a frame and see the outline of a region, it is still posterized.
3. **Solid black is the last resort.** By day almost nothing is solid. At night the sky and what your lamp hasn't touched can be, and where solid meets light it feathers as in 18.
4. **Marks follow the form.** Round things are hatched round. Walls along the wall. Ground along the ground. Light decides how many marks; the surface decides which way they go. A pump globe should read as a ball from its hatching alone.
5. **Every material has its own texture.** Stucco, concrete slab, blacktop, painted steel, glass, wood, tin, dirt. Someone should be able to tell the wall from the canopy from the pump with the light the same on all three. This is my best reading of "texturing"; the orchestrator is asking him what "detail boss texturing" meant.
6. **Line with weight.** Contours as Kirby inks them: thick where the form turns away from the light, thin or broken where it faces it. Never a hairline of constant width. This replaces last round's edge line.
7. **More to see the closer you get.** At every distance there is detail at the scale of that distance. Walk up to a pump and find its dial, its hose, its rivets, its wear. Nothing should be a flat fill at arm's length.
8. **Still his other words.** Monocolour. Brush and ink, "readable but not precise or always whole": the hatching is by a hand with a brush or pen, uneven, some lines missing, not a mechanical screen pattern. "Liminal not funny": density must not turn the lot into a busy comic panel. The sky by day stays empty. The quiet is in how much is left blank, the richness is in what's drawn.
9. **Smooth at the pixel.** An ink line on his phone has to look like ink on paper, not like stair-steps. Smoothing the edge of a line is not "grey"; "pure ink" is about tone, and I read it wrongly as a rule about pixels. Whatever caused the blockiness goes, including any reduced-resolution fallback that turns on by itself. I asked for that fallback last round. It may be part of what he saw.

A test you can run on any frame: put it beside 16, 17 and 13 at the same size on a phone screen. Does it belong with them? Then take a photograph of a gas station and reduce it to two levels in an image editor. If our frame looks more like that than like his references, it fails.

## What must be checked on a real phone, not in a headless browser

Everything I got wrong, I got wrong by looking at 390 × 844 at low density with software rendering.

- **Sharpness at the phone's real pixel density.** Lines and hatching crisp, no stair-steps, no enlarged pixels.
- **Fine marks in motion.** Hatching shimmers and makes moiré patterns when it is finer than the screen can show. Walk and turn; watch the hatching on far surfaces.
- **Press and hold.** No copy and paste callout, no text selection, no magnifier, no image-save sheet, no context menu. Also double-tap doesn't zoom, a drag down doesn't refresh the page, a drag from the edge doesn't go back, and the browser's bars appearing and hiding don't jump the picture.
- **Frame rate at full resolution** with the richer drawing, and whether the phone gets hot.
- **Both ways up**, and with the phone's own text size and zoom settings changed.
- **Outdoors brightness.** Fine line on a phone in daylight.
- **The handover of marks as you walk in:** focus or crawl. Still unseen by anyone but him, and he hasn't said.

A headless browser can pretend to be dense and to have touch. It cannot show a long-press callout, shimmer, heat, or what a thumb does. Treat anything checked only that way as unchecked.
