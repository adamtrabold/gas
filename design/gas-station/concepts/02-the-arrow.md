# 02 — The Arrow

**You are** someone following a shadow across a forecourt.
**The hour** lays a path from the sign and wakes whatever the path points at.

*How it works (systems), turn 2. Reworked on the terms in `steer-1.md` section 3. Read `00-shared.md` first.*

**Draws on:** the director's sundial and "The Instrument"; the visual designer's sentence, "the arrow's shadow is the path the day has laid for you."
**Kept:** a real sun, nothing stretched; it has to work for someone who never thinks "dial".
**Changed since turn 1:** the raked dart is gone; the sign is the upright pylon every concept has. No joints as hour lines, no table of seven. Five things can be awake, none of them indoors. The sun patch is no longer a hand. The inside has a different job.
**Dropped:** fourteen fuller answers; the stars as a clock with a purpose; the door ajar at noon as a rule (it survives as one of five).

## My view, before the detail

The steer said that if I conclude it folds, I should say so. I think **it half folds.**

The path is real in every concept. Ink is shadow, the sign is twelve metres tall, so a band of ink crosses the lot all day in 01 and 03 as well, and people will follow it there too. That part isn't 02's to own, and I've put the lot's layout in the shared file for that reason.

What only 02 has is **"awake"**: the hour picks one thing and it behaves differently. That is a real answer to the owner's open question, and it's cheap, five fuller answers and a rule. So I'd keep 02 as the way to show him "the hour does something", and be plain with him that it is 01's place with one rule added, not a third world. If the director wants three that each stand as a whole world, this isn't one, and it should fold. I've written it up fully so that either call can be made from the page.

## Where the path really lies

A 12 m sign at the road, the door 16 m straight north of it, a generic desert sun (35°N). Worked, not guessed:

| | Morning (9–10) | Midday | Afternoon (2–3) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Spring and autumn** | 12–17 m, to the north-west: in under the canopy, to the pumps | 8 m, straight at the door; stops halfway | 12–17 m, north-east: off the slab toward the phone box |
| **Midsummer** | 6–10 m, due west along the roadside | 2.5 m. A stub at the foot of the sign | 6–10 m, due east along the roadside |
| **Midwinter** | 26–38 m, north-west, across the canopy and up the garage door | 19.5 m: all the way to the door, and 2 m up the wall | 26–38 m, north-east, up the wall past the bench |

What that gives, honestly:

- **Morning it leads toward the pumps. Midday it leads to the door. Afternoon it leads away from the building.** Three destinations. Not seven, and not a dial.
- **In summer it barely leads anywhere.** A stub at noon; along the kerb at the ends of the day, pointing down the road. Follow it down the road and you walk out of the world and arrive again (shared: where the world ends). So when the path ends on nothing, it ends on the road, and the road ends on the station.
- **In winter it does something the other seasons can't.** The band reaches the front wall and stands up on it. From about November to January, around midday, the shadow of the top of the sign, starburst and arrowhead, is on the wall by the door at eye height, where a person walking can actually read it as an arrow. On the shortest day the starburst is on the door.
- At the very ends of the day the band is 50 m long and leaves the lot altogether.

## Awake

Five things, all outdoors, each on a bearing from the sign:

| Thing | Where | Awake when the band points at it |
|---|---|---|
| West pump | Under the canopy | Mid-morning |
| Air post | Edge of the canopy | Late morning |
| The door | Straight ahead | Midday, all year |
| Bench | By the wall, east of the door | Early afternoon |
| Phone box | Alone, off the slab to the east | Late afternoon |

**The rule:** the thing nearest the line of the band, if it's within a hand's width of it, is awake. If nothing is, nothing is awake. It's the direction that counts, not whether the tip reaches, so in summer the door is still awake at noon with the stub pointing at it from fourteen metres off.

The hours shift with the season by up to an hour, because the sun does. Between any two things there's a stretch of the day, twenty minutes to an hour, with nothing awake. I'd leave both. They're true.

**What awake is:**

- It has its solid blacks without you standing at it. Everything else on the lot is paint and thin shadow.
- It makes its sound by itself, from where it is: the pump ticks, the air post hisses, the phone box hums. You hear it before you've worked anything out, and the sound is to one side, so you turn.
- Call gives the answer it only has now. The pump's numbers run to a figure and stop. The phone in the box is already off the hook.
- **The door, awake, is off the latch.** At midday you walk straight in. The one hour the place lets you in without asking.

**Kill condition, as the director set it:** walking at eye height on a phone, can you tell which thing is awake without being told? I think yes for the pump, the door and the phone box, because each is black, sounding, and at the end of a dark band you're standing in. I'm not sure for the air post and the bench: they're small. If visual's frames say no, cut to three.

## Night and twilight

At night there's no path. The arrow's bulbs run down to the door and the pool at the door is the only lit ground. The door is awake all night; whether it's also off the latch all night is a choice. I'd say no: the latch at night, the open door at noon.

At twilight nothing is awake and there's no path and no bulbs. Shared with the others.

## The inside's job

Indoors is where you see the whole thing and where you work it.

- **The window is a picture of the lot.** By day the room is ink and the window is paper, so it hangs on the wall like a framed print: the sign, the slab, the band lying across it. It's the one fixed view in a walked world, and a fixed view is what you need to see something move slowly.
- **The clock is beside the window, in reach from the same spot.** Turn it and the band swings across the picture, the black jumps from thing to thing on the lot, and their sounds come and go through the glass.
- **Then go out and it's still wound.** The path you set is the path that's there. Walk it.
- The sun patch on the floor is just a patch of sun.

So the room is the instrument's case: the face is the window and the hand is on the wall. That's a job, and no other concept's room has it. Whether it's enough of a room is the director's call; it has the counter and the aisle from 01 behind it, unchanged, and I haven't given those anything new.

## The alien material

- **A second band.** Now and then, for a minute, there's another shadow on the slab at the wrong angle for the sun, with nothing above it. It points at something too. That thing is awake as well.
- The shortest-day alignment on the door is not alien; it's the sun. But it's the thing here you'd tell someone about.
- The three circles (shared) lie out east, past the phone box, where the summer evening shadow points and nothing else is.

## Doing nothing

Stand in the band. In a couple of minutes its edge has moved off your feet. Stand at the window and it's a clock. Wait here gives less than in 01 or 03, and I'd say so to the owner.

## States particular to this concept

- **Midsummer noon:** a stub and an unlatched door. The thinnest hour; the door still works.
- **Clock wound at night:** you turn it to morning and the sun comes up outside the window. The margin says wound.
- **Standing in the band when the clock is turned:** it sweeps off you. Nothing else happens to you.
- **No 3D:** one line under "The road": the time, where the shadow lies, what it's pointing at.
- **Sound off:** the awake thing's sound becomes a small mark repeating beside it.

# 02 — The Arrow: how it looks and feels

*Visual turn 1. Read the look-and-feel half of `00-shared.md` first. Pictures: `stills/sheet-02.png`, `stills/02a.png`–`02d.png`.*

**Draws on:** Ruscha's Standard Station (03) for the diagonal; the Googie references (12) for the sign; Kirby's foreshortening (16, 17) for the view from under it; the owner's brush image 14 for shadows as stacks of strokes.

## The question I was asked: can a Route 66 arrow sign rake over the forecourt?

**Yes, as two parts** (`02b`):

- **The name stays upright.** A pylon with TRABOLD in lit boxes stacked down it, facing the road. This is the Route 66 part and it reads from anywhere.
- **The arrow rakes over it**, from a low tail near the road up over the forecourt, with a row of bulbs along each edge. This is the Googie part.

Three things I learned drawing it:

1. **It can't be a flat board.** My first version was a flat arrow lying in the raked plane. From the road you look along that plane, so the arrowhead was a sliver. The arrow has to be a solid dart: a beam with a spike on the end, which reads as an arrowhead from any side. It still throws an arrow-shaped shadow.
2. **It points up and away, not down at the door.** Arrow signs of that era usually dive at the business. This one climbs past it into the sky. In `02b` it still reads as "that way" because it recedes over the building. I think the oddness helps a liminal place, and it is true to what the arrow is for. But it is the less usual direction, and the owner's saved references should be checked by someone who can open them.
3. **It is big.** About 20 m long with the tip 11 m up. A smaller one throws a hand too short to reach anything.

So: not a piece of apparatus, in my judgement, as long as the name is on its own upright pylon. If the name had to ride on the arrow it would fail.

## The look

A station with a canopy over the pumps on the right, and the door standing in the open on the left. The door has to be clear of the canopy, because anything under a canopy is in shade and the arrow's shadow can't be seen there. The slab's joints are thin broken lines fanning from the foot of the arrow. They read as cracks.

## The five moments

| Moment | Picture | What you see and feel |
|---|---|---|
| Arriving | `02b` | Noon. Arrow in the air, its shadow on the ground, both ending at the door. The door stands ajar. This is the best day picture of the four concepts |
| On the forecourt | `02a` | The arrow is behind and above you. Its shadow comes past your feet as a band and ends on the air post, which is the one thing here in solid black |
| Crossing the door | as `01b` | The same stroke. At noon there is no latch and no word |
| Inside | `02d` | The room is ink; a patch of sun is the hand. **This picture fails.** The patch is a sliver and you can't tell what it's on |
| Another hour | `02c` | Night, from the foot of the arrow. Bulbs run up the shaft. One pool of paper, at the door. The sky turns round a point just past the tip |

## What the drawing found

- **From eye height the shadow reads as a band, not as an arrow.** Near you it is a wide dark path (`02a`, `02b`). Its head, 20 m away, is a few pixels tall. So "a big arrow lying on the ground with its head on a pump" is not what a visitor at eye height sees. They see a dark path leading somewhere.
- **So "awake" has to be carried by the thing.** Solid black where everything else is paper and line, and its sound. The air post in `02a` does this. The shadow's job is to lead you to it.
- **Things are too small to read from the road on a phone.** The awake thing is about 20 pixels wide from the arrival spot. You have to go to it.
- **The real sun moves the head a long way with the season.** With the arrow as drawn, the head reaches the threshold at noon around the equinoxes, climbs the door in winter, and stops short in the open lot in summer. On winter mornings it is off the lot altogether and only the band crosses it. All honest, none of it stretched. But systems' table of seven things by hour can't be laid out once; it has to be worked from the geometry, and some hours will point at bare concrete.
- **Morning and evening the shadow is out of a tall frame** unless you turn toward it.

## With "walk around": the shadow is a path

This is the concept walking helps most, and it fixes the two findings above. If you can walk, **the band on the ground is something to follow.** It starts near where you arrive and ends at the awake thing. You don't have to understand a dial or read an arrowhead; you walk along a shadow and arrive at the thing that's ticking.

I'd reshape the concept round that sentence: *the arrow's shadow is the path the day has laid for you.* At noon it leads to the door. At night there's no path, only the bulbs running toward the building and a pool of light at the door.

**Needs systems:** the things-by-hour table reworked from real shadow positions through the year; what happens at hours when the path ends on nothing; the wall clock swinging a path you might be standing on.

## Pushing on it

- **The inside is this concept's hole.** Systems said so and `02d` shows it. A sun patch through a window is small, low and mostly on the floor for much of the year. I don't have a fix. It may be that this concept is honest only outdoors, and indoors belongs to another.
- **Fourteen fuller answers** is a lot of making. With the path doing the leading, I'd start with five outdoors and none indoors.
