# Jam — gas station portfolio

## Pass 1 — systems

Written from the owner's words and the 18 inspiration images. Nothing here is decided; it's material to push on.

### 1. Jobs

Who is doing them: (a) someone deciding whether to hire or work with Adam, often in a hurry and often on a phone; (b) a peer who will wander for pleasure; (c) someone who came for the music; (d) a returning visitor; (e) Adam, keeping it current. We don't know the mix yet — that's a question for the owner.

| # | Job | From |
|---|---|---|
| J1 | Arrive outside the station and know, within a few seconds, whose place this is and that there's work inside | owner ("you start out of the gas station") + inferred |
| J2 | Go in | owner ("and can go in") |
| J3 | Find the work and see what there is to choose from | owner ("portfolio site") |
| J4 | Look at one piece properly: what it is, Adam's part in it, the images or sound | inferred |
| J5 | Find out who Adam is — designer, musician, design director | inferred |
| J6 | Get in touch | inferred |
| J7 | Come across things that weren't announced — the alien material, things that depend on the hour | owner ("Easter eggs like alike stuff and time of day stuff") |
| J8 | Come back at another hour and find the place changed | owner ("time of day stuff") |
| J9 | Get to the work without the wandering — in a hurry, on a small phone, with a screen reader, with motion turned off | inferred |
| J10 | Send someone a link to one piece, and land on that piece when you open it | inferred |
| J11 | Leave — step back out, and know where you are when you do | inferred ("liminal") |
| J12 | Hear the music | inferred (he's a musician); may not be wanted |
| J13 | Adam adds, removes or reorders work without the world being redrawn | inferred (owner as user) |

Two of these pull against each other and the design has to hold both: J7 (things hidden) and J9 (nothing in the way). My rule for the jam: **the world may hide surprises, never the work.** J3, J4, J5, J6 are never behind an egg, an hour, or a wait.

### 2. What the owner's words settle, and what they don't

Settled: a portfolio; a vintage gas station in the desert; one colour, black and white brush strokes; you begin outside and can go in; Easter eggs including hour-dependent ones; Ruscha meets Kirby's sketches; liminal; every interaction means something.

Not settled, and worth arguing about:

- **What "going in" is.** A cut, a push through the door, the drawing turning over, a door that has to be asked? (Section 4.)
- **How literal the station is.** A place you move through, one picture you operate, or a book about a station?
- **What "every interaction has artistic meaning" demands of controls.** I read it as a limit on how many kinds of action exist. (Section 5.)
- **Whether the work itself goes monochrome.** The world is one colour; Adam's work presumably isn't.

### 3. What the images say about structure

Not how they look — how they're built.

- **03, 04, 07** Ruscha puts one word in a landscape and lets it be the whole picture. A sign can carry the navigation; it doesn't need a menu bar beside it.
- **08** Every Building on the Sunset Strip: one continuous elevation, both sides of the street, the lower strip upside down. A structure, not a style: a line with two sides, read by travelling.
- **02, 10** Dead frontal, symmetrical, one door in the middle. Prada Marfa is a shop you can never enter. The owner says you *can* go in — so the door opening is the event, and it should cost something small.
- **09, 10** Nobody is there. The missing attendant is the liminal part.
- **15** A grid of drawings, each captioned with a place and a date. That's a log format, ready-made.
- **16–18** Kirby's originals show the working: margin notes, registration marks, blue pencil, paste-up. The panels in 18 are a sequence of one thing changing. "Sketches" in the owner's sentence suggests the unfinished state matters.
- **13, 14** Strokes have a start, a swell and a taper — each is a gesture with a duration. An interaction can be one stroke.

### 4. Structure

#### The places (a pool to draw from, not a sitemap)

Outside: the road, the sign on its pole, the price board, the canopy, the pumps, the air hose and bell cord, the window, the door, the sky, the horizon.
Inside: the counter and register, shelves, the cooler, a postcard rack, a magazine rack, a radio or jukebox, a payphone, a wall clock, a restroom key on a hook, a back door.
Behind: the garage bay, the lot out back, the desert.

A first mapping of content to place, to be fought over:

| Content | Candidate place | Why there |
|---|---|---|
| Who and what this is (J1) | The sign | It's what a sign is for |
| The list of work (J3, J9) | The price board | A price board is already a plain list, readable from the road |
| The work (J3, J4) | Shelves / aisles inside | Things you pick up and look at |
| About (J5) | The counter — a note left by the attendant | He's not here; he left word |
| Contact (J6) | The payphone, or the bell | The means of calling someone who isn't there |
| Music (J12) | The radio | Already the thing that plays |
| Work in progress, sketches | The garage bay | Things up on the lift |
| Eggs (J7) | Sky, restroom, back lot, the radio dial between stations | The edges |

#### Four shapes the whole thing could take

These differ in kind. Each names what "going in" becomes.

**A. The loop.** Three depths on one axis: outside → inside → out the back. Out the back is the desert again, and walking on brings you round to the front — at a different hour. You can't get lost because there's only forward and back; it's liminal because it never ends. *Going in* is one step deeper. The browser's back button is a real control here: back is always "step out". Eggs live on later laps.

**B. The two-sided strip** (from 08 and "a line has two sides"). One long horizontal drawing you travel along. The station front is one stretch of it. *Going in* is the strip turning over: the same line seen from its other side, ink and paper swapped, the window now looking out at the road you were on. Outside and inside are one drawing. Works on a phone because travel is a single axis.

**C. The interior that's bigger than the outside.** Outside is one small frontal building (02, 10). Inside, the aisles keep going — more shelf than the building could hold. *Going in* is crossing into a place with different rules. This solves J13 outright: another piece of work is another stretch of aisle, and the outside never changes. Eggs sit deep in the aisles and at the far end.

**D. Self-service.** The station is unmanned; a "back in 5 minutes" card hangs in the door. You do everything yourself, and what you find is what the attendant left. *Going in* needs you to do what you'd do at a real one — ring the bell, try the handle. Contact is the bell or the payphone. At the end the register can print a receipt of what you looked at: a plain list, with links, that you can keep or send (J10).

They combine. A is a path, C is a capacity, D is a manner, B is a geometry. My lean is A's path with C's interior and D's manner, with B as the strongest alternative if the others feel like a game level. That's a lean to argue with.

#### What must be true in any of them

1. **Every piece of work, About and Contact has its own address** (J10). Opening one lands you in the right spot in the world with the way back out visible.
2. **There is a plain list of everything**, one action from anywhere, that is itself part of the world — the price board, the receipt, or a Ruscha-style booklet on the counter ("Twentysix …"). It's also what a screen reader, a no-motion setting or a failed load gets (J9). Ruscha's books are deadpan lists; a list here is in character, not a compromise.
3. **Arriving cold shows the station and the name before anything moves** (J1). No intro to sit through.
4. **Adding work never needs a new drawing of the world** (J13). Work fills repeatable slots: a shelf item, a board line, a strip segment.

### 5. Interaction ideas

#### A small set of gestures

"Every interaction has artistic meaning" fails the moment there are thirty kinds of clickable thing. Proposal: five gestures, each one brush stroke long, each meaning one thing.

| Gesture | What you do | What it means | What the ink does |
|---|---|---|---|
| Look | Point at or focus something | Attention | Ink gathers there; the rest thins |
| Cross | Go through a door or window | Changing sides | Paper and ink swap, or the frame pushes through |
| Pick up | Take one thing off a shelf | Choosing | The world steps back; one thing at a time |
| Call | Ring the bell, lift the phone, honk | Asking for someone who isn't there | A word lands in the picture (04: HONK) |
| Wait | Do nothing | Time passing | The light moves; things arrive |

Test for anything we add: say what it means in one line. If we can't, it goes.

"Wait" is the one I'd protect. Doing nothing is an input almost no site honours, and it's the most liminal one: stay a minute and the shadows shift, a car passes, something crosses the sky. It must never gate the work.

Phones have no pointer to hover with, so Look has to work as a first tap or as whatever sits at the centre of the screen. Flagging for the visual designer: the station is wide and a phone is tall; the sign on its pole is the one element that's naturally tall.

#### One colour gives exactly two states

Black on white, or white on black. That's a switch the whole system can run on:

- Day is ink on paper; night is paper on ink. Time of day costs one swap, not a second set of drawings.
- Or: outside is ink on paper, inside is the reverse. Crossing the door is the swap.
- It can't be both without confusion. One for the director to call once we see them.

#### The work and the one-colour rule

Three options: (1) the world is strictly one colour and an opened piece shows in its true colours — the only colour on the site is Adam's work; (2) work appears as ink until you pick it up, then true; (3) everything stays one colour. I'd take (2): it gives Pick up a visible consequence and keeps the shelves coherent. (3) undersells the work. This needs the owner.

#### Time of day (J8)

- Driven by the visitor's own clock. Four states is enough to notice: dawn, day, dusk, night.
- **The hour changes mood and eggs, never access.** At night the station can be "closed" in look — sign lit, door on the latch — and still open when you try it.
- Someone who only ever visits at 2pm should still be able to find the night. The wall clock inside can be wound by hand. Winding it is a Call or a Wait made visible; it's also an egg in itself.
- Borrowed from stage management: think of the day as a cue sheet. Each hour calls a few cues (light, sign, sky, what's on the radio). New eggs are new cues, added without touching structure.
- Borrowed from a planetarium: at night the sky can be a second index — each piece of work a point of light, the Kirby dots as stars. A different way into J3, only after dark, with the price board still there.

#### Easter eggs (J7)

Three kinds, so they don't all feel the same:

- **Happens to you**: a saucer crosses at night; the sign flickers; a tumbleweed. No action needed.
- **Found by poking**: the restroom key, the radio dial between stations, the payphone ringing with nobody near it, the back lot.
- **Earned over visits**: things only on a later lap, a later hour, a certain date.

And a place to keep them, straight from image 15: a **sightings chart**. Each egg you find is logged as a small line drawing with a caption — where on the site, and the date you saw it. Blank cells show how many are left without saying what. It's a reason to come back (J8), it lives in the visitor's own browser, and it's findable inside (pinned by the register) rather than announced.

If "alike" isn't "alien", the chart still works — it just logs other things.

#### Sound (J12)

Off until the visitor turns the radio on. Then the radio is a real player for Adam's music, and it keeps playing as you move about. Tuning between stations is where an egg lives. Whether music is portfolio work or atmosphere is the owner's call.

### 6. Flows

Written against no particular shape; each has to survive whichever we pick.

**First visit, curious.** Arrive outside → the sign says whose station → Look around, something responds → Cross the door → shelves → Pick up a piece → put it back → another → notice the note on the counter (About) → Call (Contact) or leave by the back → outside again, later.

**First visit, two minutes.** Arrive outside → the price board lists the work in plain words → choose a line → you're at that piece → next piece from there without going back → Contact is on the same list.

**From a shared link.** Open a link to one piece → you're inside, holding it → the way out to the shelves and to the door is visible → the rest of the world is there if wanted.

**Return visit at night.** Arrive → it's dark, the sign is lit → something is different in the sky → the door still opens → the chart by the register has a new entry.

**Adam adds a piece.** Adds one entry with its words and images → it appears on the shelf, on the board and in the list → nothing else is touched.

### 7. States to design from the start

- **Loading**: the place draws itself in strokes — but the name and the list are readable before the drawing finishes.
- **Wrong address**: empty highway, a sign reading "next services" with the way back to the station.
- **Nothing on a shelf yet / one piece only**: the shop still has to look like a shop. A nearly bare shelf is in character; it must not look broken.
- **Motion turned off**: gestures become cuts; Wait still changes the light, stepwise.
- **Keyboard and screen reader**: the plain list is the primary route, and every object in a scene is reachable in an order that makes sense.
- **Small phone, tall**; **wide desktop**; **landscape phone**.
- **Sound unavailable or blocked**: the radio is visibly off, not silent-and-on.

## Pass 1 — visual

Written after opening all the owner's images and after "I'm thinking threejs". I built one throwaway sketch to test the handoff question: `sketches/ink-is-shadow/sheet.png` (source beside it; `node render.js` redraws it). It is a crude three.js scene with a procedural stand-in for brushwork. Judge the rule it shows, not the drawing.

### 1. What three.js changes

The systems turn assumed drawings. A real-time 3D scene changes the cost of things:

- **Cheap now:** any camera position, any sun angle, shadows, depth, a place that is bigger inside. Time of day stops being "a second set of drawings".
- **Expensive now:** looking drawn by a hand. 3D's default look is smooth, shaded, grey. The owner asked for black and white brush strokes. The whole visual risk of this project is a station that looks like a filter on a 3D model.
- **Still needed:** the plain list, real text, an address per piece. A 3D canvas holds none of these by itself. They live in the page around and over the picture.

So the question I'd put first is not "which shape" but **what is a brush stroke in a 3D world?** Three answers that differ in kind:

| | What a stroke is | What it's good at | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|---|
| **S1. Ink is shadow** | The scene is solid and lit. A pixel is ink where light doesn't reach, paper where it does. Edges and mid-tones get a brush treatment | Time of day, inside/outside, Kirby's big spotted blacks (16–18), Ruscha's hard slabs (03). Everything stays consistent as you move | The hand. A shader's line is never quite a stroke. Needs real scanned brush marks feeding it, or it looks processed |
| **S2. Strokes hung in the air** | The station is a few hundred real brush marks, each a flat ribbon placed in 3D. From the arrival spot they line up into a station. Move and they drift apart into marks (14 is this already: strokes that only suggest a shape together) | The hand is real: every mark is a scan of an actual stroke. "A place that is only a place from where you stand" is the liminal idea made visible. Loading is the strokes arriving | Interiors, small objects, anything you need to read from many angles. Every object is hand-placed, so J13 needs slots |
| **S3. Flats** | Drawings on flat sheets standing in the desert, like a film set or a paste-up (08, 16). Walk past and the station is paper-thin. Prada Marfa (10) is a facade | Cheapest. Fully hand-drawn. Honest about being a picture | Uses almost nothing 3D offers. "Go in" becomes a cut |

My lean: **S1 as the rule of the world, S2 for the things that happen in it.** The still world is lit geometry rendered as ink; events (the door, a Call, an egg, loading) are real strokes laid over it. That split is also the owner's sentence, see section 4.

### 2. The handoff question, tested: does the ink/paper swap mean night, or inside?

The systems turn said it can't be both. The sketch says it can, because both are the same fact. One rule: **ink is where the light isn't.**

| | Outside | Inside |
|---|---|---|
| **Day** | Paper. The sun is on everything; ink is the shadow under the canopy (frame 1) | Ink. The room is dark against the glare; door and windows are the paper (frame 5) |
| **Night** | Ink. Paper is only where the canopy lamp and shop light land (frame 4) | Paper. A lit room; the doorway is black (frame 6) |

- **Crossing the door always turns the page over, at any hour.** By day you step from paper into ink; at night from ink into paper. Nobody has to learn a rule; it's how a doorway looks.
- **Night is not an invert.** A plain invert reads as a photographic negative. Night is the same scene with the sun off and the station's own lamps on. In frame 4 the shelves show through the window from across the lot: the work is the lit thing in the dark, which is the right thing for a portfolio to be.
- **Time of day is one number**, the sun's angle. Dawn and dusk are long shadows, noon is almost no ink at all, night is the lamps. The systems designer's "wait" gesture is the shadow creeping. Coming round the loop "later" is the same station with the ink moved.
- **A problem the sketch found:** inside by day, the shelves vanish into the black (frame 5). The work must never sit in the dark. So the things that hold work need their own light: a cooler that glows, a lamp over the rack, the sun patch falling on the counter. That turns "where is the light inside" into the layout question for the interior. Good constraint.

### 3. The handoff question, tested: a wide station on a tall phone

Frame 2 is the failure: frontal camera, tall screen, the station is a stripe and the sign is out of shot. Three ways out, all from the owner's images:

1. **Give the sky the job (04, 06, 07).** Ruscha's picture is a word with a landscape under it. On a tall phone the station sits in the bottom quarter and the name fills the sky as letters standing in the scene (frame 2b, type pasted on flat for proportion only). 07 is this exact layout: a wide picture, a lot of nothing, small text. The emptiness is the liminal part, so the phone may be the better version.
2. **The Ruscha corner (03).** Camera on the ground at the corner so the canopy runs up the page and the sign pole is the vertical. Frames 3 and 4. It works as a rule (same scene, only the camera moved) but my framing is weak: the station still sits low and the sign is cropped. It needs composing by hand, and the station may need designing from this corner first, the way Ruscha's is.
3. **The page of panels (18).** Kirby's page is tall panels in tiers. A phone screen is a comic page: a tall panel for the sign, a wide one for the station, a strip for the ground with the list in it. Each panel is a different camera on the same live scene. Desktop is the splash page. Going in is crossing a gutter.

I'd take 1 for arrival and keep 3 alive as a whole direction, because it gives the phone its own form instead of a cropped desktop. 2 is a camera move to use, not a layout.

**Against the four shapes:**

- **A (the loop):** carries well. A lap is a camera on a rail, and "later" is the sun having moved.
- **B (the two-sided strip):** weakest with three.js and with a tall phone; a horizontal strip in a tall frame is a ribbon with dead space. I'd stop treating it as the world and keep it as the form of the plain list: every piece as a frontage on one long paste-up strip (08). Proposing that, not deciding it.
- **C (bigger inside):** strongest. Outside is wide and low; an aisle is tall and deep, one-point perspective running away like the street in 16. A tall phone suits an aisle. The phone problem only exists outside.
- **D (self-service):** a manner, carried by lettering: the attendant's notes are in a hand, everything else is signage.

### 4. A grammar from the owner's sentence: Ruscha is the stillness, Kirby is the event

"Ed Ruscha meets Jack Kirby's sketches" could be a blend. Sharper as a split:

- **The world at rest is Ruscha.** Deadpan, empty, level horizon, one word, hard slabs of black, nothing moving. No wobble, no line boil. Stillness is where the liminal feeling comes from.
- **Anything that happens is Kirby.** A response to you arrives as an inked mark with force: speed lines, a burst, krackle dots (18), a foreshortened shape coming at the camera (the fist in 16), a slab word landing (04's HONK in Kirby's lettering energy).

Every interaction is then the moment the still picture gets drawn on, and the mark fades back to stillness. That's a visual meaning for "every interaction has artistic meaning" and it tells the builder when things may move: only when something happened.

**Krackle is the alien material.** Kirby's dots are his sign for energy that isn't from here. They are the stars at night, radio static between stations, the edge of the saucer's light, and the trace left where an egg was found. Nothing else in the world is made of dots, so dots always mean "not from here".

**"Sketches" is the frame.** The originals (16–18) show blue pencil, margin notes, pinholes, registration marks, a title scrawled on top. So the screen is an art board, and the margin is where the site's plain controls live: the list, sound, the way out, written as production notes outside the picture. Controls never have to be disguised as objects, and the picture never has a menu bar on it.

### 5. The five gestures, each as one stroke

| Gesture | The mark |
|---|---|
| Look | The thing gets its blacks. At rest it's outline and shadow; under attention its solid black fills in, one loaded stroke, swell then taper (14) |
| Cross | One full-width stroke dragged across the screen; behind the brush is the other side of the page. With motion off: a cut |
| Pick up | The thing lifts off as a cut-out with a hard paste-up shadow; the world drops back to outline; the piece shows in its true form |
| Call | A slab word lands in the scene, extruded, casting its own ink (04). DING. HONK. Then it stays a while, like a sign |
| Wait | Nothing is drawn. The shadows move |

One I'd add to argue over: **attention is the inker.** Kirby pencilled, someone else inked. Detail in the world sits in thin pencil line until looked at, then it's inked, and it stays inked on your next visit. A returning visitor's station is more finished than a stranger's, and the visited state is the drawing itself. Risk: a first-time station that looks faint. Structure would have to be fully inked from the start; only detail waits.

### 6. Tone, type, colour

- **Strictly two values.** Every pixel is ink or paper; no grey, no opacity fades. Greys come from how ink behaves: dry-brush break-up (13), stipple and grain (07), krackle. Fades become a brush running out of ink. This single rule does most of the work of making 3D look printed.
- **Paper is not white.** 13 and the Kirby boards are on toned stock. Warm paper, near-black ink. Still "black and white" to the eye, far less like a screen.
- **Three voices of type, each with a job.** A slab for anything the station says (the sign, landed words; 04 and 07 are both slabs). A brush hand for anything the attendant says (notes, About). A typewriter face for anything recorded (the list, captions, the sightings chart; 15 is typewritten). Actual faces not chosen. Reading text is real page text on paper, never a texture in the scene.
- **Signs are objects.** Words stand in the desert with thickness and cast shadows, so they obey the ink rule: at noon the name is a thin outline, at dusk it throws a long black.
- **The work in colour:** I side with systems' option (2), with a mechanism. On the shelf every piece is its own image crushed to two values by the same treatment as the world, so adding a piece needs no new drawing (J13). Picked up, it's shown true. Adam's work is the only colour on the site. Needs the owner.

### 7. Motion and feel

- At rest nothing moves except heat shimmer on the horizon by day and the krackle of stars at night.
- Events are held like drawn animation, a few frames each, not eased like interface motion. A stroke lands in three or four held frames.
- The camera is the open question: smooth travel makes a shader world look most like a 3D model. Options are a camera that steps between composed views (each stop a Ruscha picture), or smooth travel with the ink settling when you stop. I lean stepped views: a small number of composed pictures, each good enough to print, beats free roaming. It is also what makes phones, keyboards and reduced motion simple.
- Sound is the other half of feel and is silent until the radio; agreeing with systems.

### 8. Two real random draws, used

Drawn with `shuf`. An unrelated field: **letterpress printing.** An Oblique Strategy: **"Emphasize the flaws."**

- From printing: the world is pulled like a print, in passes. Loading is the passes going down in order: pencil key-line, then the solid blacks, then the dry brush. Name and list are readable at pass one. Slight misregistration between passes is the texture, and registration marks sit in the margin.
- From the strategy: 3D's flaw here is that flat marks give themselves away when you move. S2 makes that the point. And the shader's flaw, that it can't hold a line everywhere, becomes the brush running dry: lines that skip, on purpose and under control.

### 9. Dropped

- A constant hand-drawn wobble on everything. It's the first idea, it fights the stillness, and it reads as a filter.
- Plain invert as night. Tested; reads as a negative.
- Halftone dots for grey. Dots are reserved for the alien material.
- Free first-person walking with a joystick. It's a game level, and it's the hardest thing to make look drawn.

### 10. What I need from the owner (adding to systems' list)

1. Will Adam make the brush marks himself? A sheet of forty or so real strokes, scanned, would be the source for every line and edge. Without a real hand the look can't get past "processed".
2. Stepped, composed views, or free movement? "Threejs" could mean either.
3. Is toned paper acceptable as "white"?

### 11. For the director

- Is "ink is where the light isn't" the foundation? It settles night, inside, time of day and the loop in one move, but it commits us to a lit 3D world rather than drawn sheets.
- Ruscha for stillness, Kirby for events: a real grammar, or too tidy?
- On the phone: big sky with the word, or the page of panels? They lead to different sites.
- Whether B stops being a world and becomes the list.
